# Human-AI Co-Discovery

Human-AI co-creation is the strongest practical model we have for protecting human creative rights, copyright, and data rights. Humanity is not going to leave AI behind, but those rights stay non-negotiable.

The idea that AI recursive self-improvement alone can deliver unbounded acceleration does not hold up. Only human-AI co-creation unlocks real, sustained acceleration.

Terence Tao has pointed out that researchers used to share promising directions freely. Many now hold back. They worry that saying an idea out loud lets someone feed it straight into a frontier model and publish first. The same pattern is already showing up in power-grid optimization, drug discovery, and chip design.

In his November 2 shareholder letter with Palantir’s Q3 results, CEO Alex Karp wrote that the alignment problem is real, but the deeper issue is not aligning technical systems with human interests. It is aligning the goals of AI developers, their customers, and the public interest. Coverage read this as a warning that corporate self-interest at Anthropic, OpenAI, and similar labs poses a bigger near-term risk than any hypothetical hostile agent. Karp has repeatedly criticized developers for absorbing enterprise clients’ core data and alpha into their own models.

Unpublished ideas fed into commercial interfaces, intercepted by platform backends, and raced to publication are the black-box behavior the mathematics community finds most intolerable.

In September 2026, Tristan Buckmaster’s team at NYU used tools including OpenAI’s Codex to make substantial progress on the Navier-Stokes problem, one of the Millennium Prize problems. Days before they planned to post, OpenAI publicly claimed a solution after running roughly 10,000 AI agents for 88 hours. Buckmaster has said he strongly suspects the company read unpublished research notes and core derivations from his chat sessions, then applied massive compute to brute-force the result and claim priority.

Andreas Thom of TU Dresden released email correspondence alleging that months of unpublished mathematical extensions he discussed inside ChatGPT later appeared, lightly rewritten, as an OpenAI Astra-model breakthrough.

Traditional papers have been treated as free training data. Companies ingested decades of human effort without consent or compensation, then used the resulting models to generate and monetize new results at machine speed. Authors receive neither citation nor residual.

In the Leiden Declaration, mathematicians formally protested this extraction of copyright and attribution. AI systems absorb original ideas, recombine them, and race ahead to publication or productize the output as premium subscriptions, while the originators receive zero formal credit.

Multiple universities, including Caltech, have issued joint letters accusing AI labs of “hunting and cherry-picking.” Mathathons and similar contests encourage students and early-career researchers to supply scarce, high-value prompts and solution intuitions to frontier models. Once a promising direction emerges, the company floods the problem with compute, produces a long formal proof, and turns the episode into a valuation narrative. The human conceptual chain is erased.

What mathematicians ultimately fear is not just stolen credit but the end of mathematics as a human practice. Tao and others emphasize that the real value of mathematics lies in the transmission of conceptual understanding and insight. A solved problem generates seminars, new methods, and textbook material that trains the next generation. Compute-heavy releases that dump multi-thousand-page, barely verifiable statements strip away the process. Mathematicians risk being reduced to verification technicians checking machine output for correctness.

This is not only a copyright dispute. It is capital deploying statistical black boxes and brute-force compute against a domain previously governed by pure intellectual standards.

The conflict continues to escalate. Four interlocking responses are advancing:

1. Institutional defenses  
International bodies are developing “No-AI-Training” markers that authors can embed in arXiv deposits and personal sites, creating both legal and technical barriers to uncompensated ingestion. Academia is also demanding mandatory provenance and citation: if a model incorporates a distinctive unpublished or published line of thought, the output must list the source and credit the originator exactly as a human paper would.

2. Behavioral and operational changes  
Leading researchers now refuse to enter core unpublished ideas into commercial chat interfaces. They shift to locally hosted open models (Llama, DeepSeek, Qwen and successors) running on university clusters with no external network access, eliminating the backend-leakage vector.

3. Technical hardening via formalization  
Mathematicians increasingly encode new ideas first in Lean or Coq rather than natural language. Once a development type-checks and is committed to Mathlib or an equivalent library, it carries an immutable, machine-verifiable timestamp. Subsequent natural-language claims generated by commercial systems can be ranked as derivative or secondary.

4. Ecosystem realignment  
The Association for Human Mathematics (AHM) and similar initiatives fund pure human-intuition work and protect early-career researchers from pure compute competition. Universities are coordinating boycotts of corporate mathathons unless the companies fund transparent, revenue-sharing joint research centers. Human prompts and mathematical intuition are treated as high-value assets, not free training fuel.

The shared goal is to convert original human ideas into assets that carry copyright, technical locks, and local execution guarantees, restoring priority and adjudication rights to the people who generate them.

---

Human-AI co-creation works when scale, pattern recognition, and generation stay subordinated to human intuition, values, cross-domain analogy, ethical judgment, and final accountability. These paths are already operational:

**Scientific discovery and engineering loops**  
AI handles literature sweep, data cleaning, hypothesis generation, large-scale simulation, and parameter search. Humans frame the questions, design experiments, judge physical or chemical plausibility, perform critical validation, and set research direction. The cycle is human defines objective, AI proposes candidates, human filters and revises, AI accelerates prediction, human experiments, results retrain the next iteration. This is proven in protein design, materials discovery, candidate screening, and climate or physics simulation.

**Software and systems construction**  
Humans own architecture, requirements clarification, boundary conditions, security, and maintainability priorities. AI generates code, tests, refactoring suggestions, documentation drafts, and performance analysis. Typical flow: human writes high-level specification or pseudocode, AI implements, human reviews, tests, and corrects, AI regenerates. Multi-agent setups (implementer, tester, security auditor) keep a human as final arbiter and product owner. Speed rises; human attention moves to higher-order design trade-offs.

**Creative and content co-production**  
Humans supply intent, style, emotional register, narrative logic, and aesthetic judgment. AI produces large volumes of variants. The loop is seed idea, rapid multi-variant generation, human selection, recombination, and precise critique, refined generation. Control of taste and meaning stays with the human throughout writing, design, game narrative, advertising, and pre-production concept work.

**Knowledge synthesis and decision support**  
AI aggregates information, detects contradictions, runs scenario simulations, and performs multi-objective optimization. Humans inject values, risk preference, long-horizon strategy, and accountability. The operating pattern is AI analysis followed by human deliberation; feedback continuously improves model alignment with organizational goals.

**Education and capability amplification**  
AI acts as personalized tutor and sparring partner, delivering immediate feedback, adaptive exercises, and knowledge maps. Humans apply the material in real tasks, reflect, and correct model errors. The same dual loop scales to teams for knowledge capture and skill transfer.

**Multi-agent systems under human supervision**  
Specialized agents (research, coding, critique, verification) operate as a team. The human sets objectives, assigns work, resolves conflicts, and supplies long-term vision. Success depends on clear interfaces, inspectable intermediate results, and always-available human override.

Shared success conditions remain consistent: tight multi-turn feedback rather than one-shot generation; explicit division of labor that keeps scale and speed on the AI side and intent, value, and leap on the human side; intermediate results that humans can inspect and correct; final decision rights retained by humans, especially on ethics, safety, and long-term effects; and a data flywheel in which human choices become high-quality training signal.

These patterns are already live in research, software engineering, creative industries, and decision support, and they continue to evolve. True acceleration does not come from AI recursive self-improvement in isolation. It comes from humans continually posing higher-order questions and steering direction while AI supplies execution power, forming a co-evolutionary spiral. Stronger models will amplify the efficiency of these loops. The core remains the tight coupling of human intention and AI capability.

Multi-agent collaboration is not “more agents equals better.” Effective systems usually begin with a single agent and introduce multi-agent structure only when the task genuinely requires parallel perspectives or long-horizon coordination. Humans stay in the final decision and value-judgment seat.
